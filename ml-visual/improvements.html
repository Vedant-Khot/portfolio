🔍 Architectural Gaps: What They Did That We Missed

1. Coxnet Survival backbone (78% weight) vs Classifier Focus

Our primary models relied heavily on a **CatBoost classifier** trained to predict event flags at discretized horizons. While we layered physics gates and calibration on top to respect time-to-event relationships, a classifier remains mathematically blind to the underlying censoring mechanism.

The 2nd place team constructed their blend with a **Coxnet survival backbone comprising 78% of the final weight**. Coxnet is a penalized Cox Proportional Hazards model which natively processes right-censored data, calculating continuous hazard ratios mathematically correctly rather than treating censored fires as negative events.

2. Specialized Close-Fire Model (<5km distance threshold)

Fires occurring close to evacuation boundaries behave completely differently from distant forest fires. In our design, we built a Two-Brain architecture segmented by perimeter snapshot counts (low-res vs high-res data availability).

The 2nd place solution instead split models directly by **geospatial proximity**. They built a specialized Close-Fire Coxnet model trained only on the 69 fires starting within 5 km of evacuation zones. Crucially, they set a high regularization (`alpha=0.20` vs backbone `0.05`) to prevent overfitting on this tiny 69-sample subset while allowing the model to adapt specifically to extreme-risk kinematics.

3. Grid-Searched Beta ($\beta = 0.55$) Blending Weight

When combining the close-fire specialized model with the primary backbone model, the V28 solution used a dedicated blending parameter $\beta = 0.55$: $$\text{Probability}_{\text{final}} = \beta \cdot P_{\text{close}} + (1 - \beta) \cdot P_{\text{backbone}}$$

Rather than using manual heuristic blend ratios (which we applied globally to submission outputs), the 2nd place team performed a **13-value grid search** over the range $[0.00, 0.60]$ to mathematically optimize the boundary transition probability weight.

4. Dual Calibration — Platt at 12h/24h vs Isotonic at 48h/72h

Calibration maps raw ML risk scores to real physical probabilities. We applied **isotonic regression across all four time horizons**.

The 2nd place team noticed a critical physical distinction between early and late horizons: - **Early Horizons (12h, 24h)**: Noisy and highly uncertain. They applied **Platt calibration (logistic regression S-curve)** to smooth out noise and avoid extreme 0 or 1 predictions. - **Late Horizons (48h, 72h)**: Physically deterministic. At 3 days, fires either clearly breached or fizzled out. They used **Isotonic calibration (step-wise step function)** to capture the sharp physical threshold cliff.

5. Circular Temporal Encoding & Interaction Features

Wildfire risk changes dynamically with time (months represent seasons, hours represent daily peak burn periods). We fed raw integers (e.g. Month 1 to 12) into models, which creates an artificial discontinuity between December (12) and January (1).

The 2nd place solution resolved this using **Circular Encoding (sine/cosine transformation)**: $$\text{month}_{\sin} = \sin\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot \text{month}}{12}\right), \quad \text{month}_{\cos} = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot \text{month}}{12}\right)$$ This positions time features on a continuous circle where December and January map adjacent to each other. They then coupled these circular timestamps with directional values to capture seasonal winds (e.g., `align_x_month_sin`).

6. 10-fold Stratified CV + 5-seed Close averaging

With a training dataset consisting of only 221 total events, validation folds are highly prone to data splitting variance. We utilized standard 5-fold cross-validation.

The 2nd place team stabilized their backbone predictions using a **10-fold cross-validation scheme**. Furthermore, for the high-risk close-fire model (69 samples), they ran **7-fold cross-validation repeated across 5 different initialization seeds** (35 total training iterations) and averaged the results. This massive oversampling stabilized the Out-Of-Fold risk boundaries and eliminated leaderboard noise.

Key Takeaway & Operational Lesson

Our primary mistake was relying on a CatBoost Classifier as the ensemble core. A proper survival analysis model (like Coxnet) respects the censoring structure of the data mathematically. While we patched this deficiency with physics-informed heuristic gates, the 2nd place team structured the model correctly from the ground up. Their iteration history shows they converged on the exact same spatial threshold we did (5km), but they possessed the correct statistical tools to exploit the boundary risk dynamics.
