This page is a deep dive into exactly what they did differently, why it worked, and what we learned from the comparison.

🔍 What They Did That We Missed

6 Key Gaps
1. Using the Right Type of AI Model for the Job
Coxnet Survival Backbone (78% weight) vs. CatBoost Classifier
High Impact
In Plain English

Imagine asking two doctors "how long will a patient survive?" Doctor A (like our CatBoost model) gives a simple yes/no answer — "will they survive past 72 hours?" Doctor B (like the Coxnet model) calculates a continuous timeline, saying "there's a 30% chance they don't make it past 24 hours, 55% past 48 hours..." That nuance matters enormously. Our model was Doctor A. The 2nd place team used Doctor B — and it showed.

Our primary models relied on a CatBoost classifier trained to predict event flags at discretized time horizons. While we layered physics gates and calibration on top to respect time-to-event relationships, a classifier remains mathematically blind to the underlying censoring mechanism — it treats "fire didn't breach in 72 hours" as a hard negative, ignoring that the fire might have breached at hour 73.

The 2nd place team constructed their blend with a Coxnet survival backbone at 78% ensemble weight. Coxnet is a penalized Cox Proportional Hazards model which natively processes right-censored data, calculating continuous hazard ratios correctly.
Modeling Paradigms: How Each Approach Handles Censored Fires
Classifier treats "no breach" as a wrong negative Survival model respects censoring — mathematically correct
2. A Specialized "Last-Mile" Model for Close Fires
Separate Coxnet for fires starting within 5km of the evacuation zone
High Impact
In Plain English

Think of it like a hospital having separate care units — a general ward and an ICU. A fire starting 50km away is managed very differently from one already inside the 5km danger zone. The 2nd place team trained a completely separate AI model just for the 69 fires that started close to evacuation areas. We split our models differently (by how much sensor data was available), which didn't capture this crucial geographic distinction.

Our Two-Brain architecture split models by perimeter snapshot counts (low-resolution vs high-resolution data availability). The 2nd place solution split models directly by geospatial proximity — building a separate Close-Fire Coxnet trained on the 69 fires starting within 5 km of evacuation zones. They used a higher regularization penalty (alpha=0.20 vs backbone 0.05) to prevent overfitting on this tiny subset while still capturing extreme-risk kinematics.
Two-Model Strategy: Splitting by Geographic Proximity
69 close fires → strict, specialized model 152 far fires → general backbone model
3. Mathematically Optimised Blending Weight
Grid-searched β = 0.55 for combining the two models
Medium Impact
In Plain English

When you have two models (the close-fire specialist and the general backbone), you need to decide how much to trust each one. We set this ratio by hand based on intuition. The 2nd place team ran 13 computer experiments, testing every possible ratio between 0% and 60%, and let the data decide the best split. Their math found β=0.55 — meaning 55% close-fire model, 45% backbone.

When combining predictions, the V28 solution used a dedicated blending parameter β = 0.55:

Final Probability = β × P(close-fire) + (1 − β) × P(backbone)

Rather than applying manual heuristic blend ratios (which we did globally), the 2nd place team performed a 13-value grid search over the range [0.00, 0.60] to mathematically optimize the boundary transition probability weight. This is a classic example of "letting the data decide" rather than human guessing.
4. Smarter Probability Calibration for Early vs Late Horizons
Platt at 12h/24h (smooth sigmoid) vs Isotonic at 48h/72h (sharp step)
Medium Impact
In Plain English

Calibration is like adjusting the confidence of a weather forecast. For short-range forecasts (tomorrow's weather), there's a lot of uncertainty — you smooth out extreme predictions. For long-range forecasts (this entire season will be either wet or dry), the reality is binary and sharp. The 2nd place team applied the appropriate calibration shape for each time horizon, while we used the same calibration technique across all four time windows.

We applied Isotonic regression across all four time horizons. The 2nd place team noticed a critical physical distinction:
• Early Horizons (12h, 24h): Noisy, uncertain → applied Platt calibration (S-curve sigmoid) to smooth out noise and avoid extreme 0 or 1 predictions
• Late Horizons (48h, 72h): Physically deterministic → at 3 days, fires either clearly breached or fizzled out → Isotonic calibration (step-wise cliff) captures the sharp physical threshold
Calibration Shapes: Smooth S-Curve (Early) vs. Step Cliff (Late)
Platt = smooth S-curve for uncertain, noisy early windows Isotonic = sharp step for clear binary outcomes at 48h/72h
5. Treating Time as a Circle, Not a Line
Circular sine/cosine encoding for months & hours
Medium Impact
In Plain English

When you represent months as numbers (January=1, December=12), an AI model sees January and December as very far apart — a gap of 11. But on a calendar, they're right next to each other. The 2nd place team encoded time as a circle using math: a sine/cosine transformation that wraps December back around to January. This is the same trick used in navigation GPS systems and music theory.

We fed raw integers (Month 1–12, Hour 0–23) into models, creating an artificial discontinuity between December (12) and January (1). The 2nd place solution resolved this using Circular Encoding:

month_sin = sin(2π × month / 12)    month_cos = cos(2π × month / 12)

This positions time on a continuous circle where December and January map adjacent. They coupled circular timestamps with directional values to capture seasonal winds (e.g., align_x_month_sin).
Raw Numbers vs. Circular Encoding — Why Continuity Matters
Linear: Dec(12) and Jan(1) appear 11 steps apart Circular: Dec and Jan are neighbours on the time ring
6. Running More Training Experiments for Stability
10-fold CV + 5-seed × 7-fold averaging on the close-fire subset
Low–Medium Impact
In Plain English

When you only have 69 data points (the close fires), a model's performance can swing wildly depending on which 69 examples you happen to use for testing versus training — like judging a chef by only tasting their food on one lucky day. The 2nd place team ran 35 separate training sessions (5 different random seeds × 7 folds) and averaged all the results for maximum stability. We used a simpler 5-fold approach.

With only 221 total events, validation folds are highly prone to data splitting variance. We utilized standard 5-fold cross-validation. The 2nd place team stabilized using:
• 10-fold cross-validation for the main backbone
• 7-fold × 5 seeds = 35 total training runs for the high-risk close-fire model (69 samples)

This massive oversampling eliminated Out-Of-Fold boundary noise and produced stable, trustworthy risk boundaries — especially critical for the tiny, high-stakes close-fire dataset.

Key Takeaway & Lesson Learned

Our primary mistake was using the wrong type of model as the foundation. A CatBoost classifier forces every wildfire into a binary bucket — "breached" or "didn't breach" — which ignores the rich timing information in the data. The 2nd place team used Coxnet, a model specifically designed for situations where you're predicting when something will happen, not just if it will happen.

The striking part? Both teams independently discovered the same 5km distance threshold as the key splitting boundary. We had the right geographic insight, but lacked the correct statistical machinery to fully exploit it. This competition taught us that domain insight and mathematical correctness both matter — neither alone is enough to reach the very top.
